How line-of-sight is calculated

For each pair of (grid square, summit), the tool traces a straight line through the terrain between the two points and measures how much clearance exists above the highest intervening obstacle. That clearance is the margin.

The terrain data comes from the Copernicus GLO-30 Digital Elevation Model (30 m native resolution), resampled to 90 m for the viewshed calculation. The study area covers Washington state with a quarter-degree buffer.

What is the margin?

The margin is the vertical clearance, in metres, between the radio path and the terrain profile along the line between the observer and the summit.

  • Positive margin (e.g. +120 m) — the path clears the terrain by that amount at its tightest point. The link is unambiguously line-of-sight.
  • Zero margin — the path just grazes the terrain.
  • Negative margin (e.g. −35 m) — the terrain blocks the straight-line path by that amount. Whether a contact is possible depends on diffraction and other propagation effects.
A negative margin does not mean a contact is impossible — VHF/UHF signals diffract over terrain ridges, and a margin of −50 m can still yield a workable path, especially on 2 m. Think of it as a confidence indicator, not a hard cutoff.

Optimistic vs. typical margin

Each 6-character Maidenhead grid square covers roughly 4 km × 5 km on the ground in Washington. You might operate from anywhere inside it, at a variety of elevations. Two margin figures capture the range:

Label Observer point used Interpretation
Optimistic (margin_max_m) Highest DEM pixel in the grid square Best-case — if you can operate from the highest hilltop in your grid
Typical (margin_mean_m) Mean elevation across the grid square Representative of a randomly-chosen operating spot

Verdicts and the default sort order use the optimistic margin. If a summit shows marginal on optimistic but the typical margin is deeply negative, you would need to find an elevated spot in your grid to have a realistic chance.

Verdict thresholds

VerdictConditionMeaning
clear margin ≥ 0 m Unobstructed line-of-sight
marginal −50 m ≤ margin < 0 m Slightly below LOS; diffraction may be enough
blocked margin < −50 m Significantly obstructed; contact unlikely on VHF

Atmospheric refraction

Radio waves follow a slightly curved path through the atmosphere, effectively bending toward the earth. This extends the radio horizon beyond the geometric horizon. The calculation uses the standard 4/3-earth model (refraction coefficient 0.857), which is a reasonable average for temperate climates. Unusual atmospheric conditions (ducting, temperature inversions) are not modelled.

Antenna heights

Both the activator (at the summit) and the chaser (at the grid square representative point) are placed 2 m above the DEM surface. This is a conservative assumption consistent with a handheld radio or a low mast. Operating from a vehicle roof or a taller mast would improve the margin slightly.

Maidenhead grid squares

This tool uses 6-character subsquares (e.g. CN86mx). Each subsquare spans 5 arcminutes of longitude × 2.5 arcminutes of latitude — roughly 6 km east-west by 4.5 km north-south in Washington state. Enter the grid in any case; it is normalised to the canonical form (AB12cd — two uppercase letters, two digits, two lowercase letters).

Distance limit

Only grid–summit pairs within 250 km are included in the database. Beyond this range, contacts on typical VHF/UHF modes are exceptional even with clear LOS.

Data sources

  • Elevation: Copernicus GLO-30 DEM via AWS Open Data
  • Summits: SOTAdata — W7W association summits list
  • Summit pages: linked to sotl.as for activation history, photos, and comments

Limitations

  • The DEM represents bare-earth or canopy elevation — dense forest can add effective terrain height that the model does not capture.
  • The observer point is the DEM maximum or mean for the whole grid square, not a specific location you choose. Use the optimistic vs. typical figures to bracket your actual situation.
  • The model does not account for buildings, power lines, or other man-made obstructions.
  • Propagation modes such as tropospheric scatter, meteor scatter, or aurora are not relevant to this LOS tool.
